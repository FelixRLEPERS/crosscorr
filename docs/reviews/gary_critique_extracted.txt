Preamble
I am a radio amateur and citizen scientist, a retired ocean technologist broadly familiar
with common statistical methods and deeply familiar with WSPR analysis.
Overall impression
The Plain Language summary is useful as the manuscript is written in a dense academic
style with many instances of statistics terminology that is unfamiliar. In places the use
of jargon that may be commonplace in statistics obscures the meaning for me.
Consequently, I suggest strongly that a professional statistician who is also a radio
amateur and familiar with WSPR be invited to comment on the appropriateness of the
statistical methods and conclusions drawn from them. I have suggested a name.
My comments are therefore primarily on the WSPR and propagation aspects of the
manuscript.
Two key questions are a) the intended readership and b) route to publication.
Comments by line numbers
Lines 13-14: An example where jargon means I do not really understand the point being
made. I can guess, but in Key Points, take the approach you did in the Plain Language
Summary. Are 20 m and 15 m ‘stable’ or not?
Line 16: You have a clear and testable hypothesis, and it is well reﬂected in the ﬁrst part
of your title. I have no doubt that the statistical methods you use lead you to robust
conclusions on the very strict and limited hypothesis you are testing. However, where
you try and explain the cause(s) of the decline and ascribe to phenomena in the
ionosphere I have several comments.
Lines 22-23: Far too conﬁdent a deduction based on your study. You do not mention
that the Kp scale is more of an exponential one rather than linear, roughly doubling in
variation in magnetic ﬂux density for each unit increase. And you conﬂate intervals 5–6
and >=6 make for a messy comparison.
Lines 26 et seq.: I could see no real justiﬁcation for the MUF being responsible. A major
weakness is that there is no discussion of why WSPR spots may not be decoded during
