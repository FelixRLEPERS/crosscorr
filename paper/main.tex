\begin{abstract}
	We test the hypothesis that geomagnetic storms ($Kp \ge 5$) suppress WSPR spot
	counts on HF amateur bands. Using openly available data from wspr.live
	(hourly aggregated, 3 bands: 20\,m, 40\,m, 15\,m) and the GFZ Potsdam $Kp$ index,
	covering 18 months (April 2024 -- September 2025; 39\,456 WSPR hours
	across the three bands, 198 storm epochs at $Kp \ge 5$), we find
	significant storm-induced spot reductions on all three bands:
	20\,m: \SI{-27.7}{\percent}, 40\,m: \SI{-27.2}{\percent},
	15\,m: \SI{-38.7}{\percent} (block permutation $p \le 2 \times 10^{-5}$
	for all, \SI{50000}{iterations}, block = 24 epochs = 72\,h).
	Because storm epochs are not evenly distributed across months and UTC
	hours, we also estimate covariate-adjusted drops on the training set
	with a negative-binomial model (month, UTC-hour and day-of-week fixed
	effects, episode-clustered errors): 20\,m \SI{-33.6}{\percent} (95\% CI
	[\SI{-39.9}{\percent}, \SI{-26.7}{\percent}]), 40\,m
	\SI{-27.1}{\percent} ([\SI{-33.2}{\percent}, \SI{-20.5}{\percent}]), and
	15\,m \SI{-39.8}{\percent} ([\SI{-46.8}{\percent}, \SI{-31.8}{\percent}]).
	The raw values are therefore conservative for 20\,m and essentially
	unchanged for 40\,m.

	We observe a scale-dependent pattern: the drop increases from
	$Kp = 5\text{--}6$ ($\sim\SI{-18}{\percent}$) to $Kp \ge 6$
	($\sim\SI{-43}{\percent}$) on 20/40\,m (15\,m: \SI{-28.6}{\percent} to
	\SI{-54.8}{\percent}), consistent with a non-linear ionospheric response
	to geomagnetic forcing. A block-permutation trend test is significant on
	all three bands ($p \le 0.02$), but the highest-$Kp$ bins are sparse
	(38 epochs from 12 episodes). A monotonic gradient is observed; the
	trend test is significant for 20\,m and 15\,m, but marginal for 40\,m
	at the episode level ($p = 0.064$), so the gradient is not resolved at
	the highest $Kp$. The primary effect replicates in direction on an independent
	held-out period (January--March 2026, updated live-database pull) on all
	three bands, including 15\,m (residual block permutation
	$p \le 2 \times 10^{-4}$; its raw 95\% CI includes zero). On the held-out
	set, covariate-adjusted drops are \SIrange{19}{23}{\percent}, smaller than
	the training values but still excluding zero.

	The frequency dependence and the exploratory UT-window pattern are
	consistent with a dual-mechanism interpretation: the 15\,m drop
	(\SI{-38.7}{\percent}) is compatible with MUF reduction, while
	D-layer absorption affects 20\,m/40\,m
	(\SI{-27.7}{\percent}/\SI{-27.2}{\percent}). The UT-window
	asymmetry (40\,m drop stronger in the 0--6 UTC window across the 8 months
	with sufficient data) is consistent with intermittent auroral and polar
	cap absorption. However, aggregate spot counts cannot uniquely separate
	these mechanisms; the mean SNR of decoded spots changes by less than
	\SI{0.5}{dB} during storms, so the count reduction is dominated by the
	loss of marginal, decodable paths rather than by a uniform SNR decrease.

	The training-period absolute spot loss on 40\,m ($\SI{-18285}{spots/h}$)
	was initially matched by a held-out snapshot ($\SI{-18304}{spots/h}$),
	but a re-downloaded held-out sample (October 2026) yields a larger
	absolute loss ($\SI{-26287}{spots/h}$; ratio 1.44, a point estimate that
	exceeds the expected scaling of $\approx$1.35, but the wide confidence
	interval [0.65, 2.33] is compatible with both proportional growth and
	invariance). Percentage drops remain the more stable metric. All held-out
	absolute-loss values are based on the updated download.
	These results suggest that WSPR can serve as a distributed ionospheric
	sensor for detecting and characterising the scale-dependent effects of
	geomagnetic storms on HF propagation.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Plain Language Summary}

Geomagnetic storms disturb the ionosphere, the atmospheric layer that
reflects high-frequency radio waves back to Earth. We tested whether a
global network of amateur radio beacons (WSPR) can detect these
disturbances. Analysing 18 months of data on three bands (20\,m, 40\,m,
15\,m), we found that during moderate-to-strong storms the number of
successfully reported signals falls by 27--39 percent, and that the
reduction grows with storm intensity. The pattern repeats in an
independent three-month period. Stronger effects on the highest band
are consistent with storm-driven reductions of the maximum usable
frequency, while the effect in the 0--6 UTC window on the lowest band is
consistent with particle-driven absorption. Because the network keeps
growing, the absolute number of lost signals may grow with the network,
but current uncertainty is large; percentage drops remain the more stable
monitoring metric. Volunteer radio data can therefore
serve as a low-cost, globally distributed sensor of space-weather
effects on radio propagation.

\section{Introduction}
%=============================================================

High-frequency (HF) radio propagation in the \SIrange{3}{30}{MHz} range relies
on ionospheric reflection from the F-layer, whose critical frequency $f_oF_2$
determines the Maximum Usable Frequency (MUF) for a given path and elevation
angle \citep{davies1990}. During geomagnetic storms, enhanced magnetospheric
convection and particle precipitation transfer energy into the ionosphere--
thermosphere system \citep{gonzalez1994,tsurutani2003}. Joule heating increases the neutral temperature, driving
composition changes that enhance molecular species (N$_2$, O$_2$) relative to
atomic oxygen, which in turn increases the recombination rate in the F2 layer
\citep{proelss1995}. The net effect is a reduction of $f_oF_2$ and a
corresponding lowering of the MUF, potentially disrupting HF communication
circuits that operate near their limiting frequency.

A second, distinct mechanism operates at lower altitudes. Energetic particle
precipitation (electrons of \SIrange{10}{300}{keV} and protons of
\SIrange{1}{30}{MeV}) penetrates into the D-region (\SIrange{60}{90}{km}),
enhancing electron density and causing non-deviative absorption of HF signals
\citep{hargreaves1969,reid1974}. Because absorption scales as $\propto
	1/f^2$, lower HF bands are disproportionately affected. During storms, this
auroral absorption and polar cap absorption (PCA) can persist for hours and
extend beyond the auroral oval.

The Weak Signal Propagation Reporter (WSPR) is a worldwide network of amateur
radio beacons and receivers that autonomously exchange low-power
(\SI{<1}{W}--\SI{5}{W}) signals on designated HF bands and report decoded
signal-to-noise ratios to a central database \citep{taylor2010}. With thousands
of stations operating continuously, the resulting dataset constitutes a
distributed, multi-frequency ionosonde that covers geographic regions and
propagation paths beyond the reach of professional sounder networks
\citep{frissell2016,labelle2023}.

Previous studies have demonstrated the utility of WSPR data for ionospheric
remote sensing. \citet{frissell2016} showed qualitative coupling between WSPR
spot patterns and geomagnetic disturbance, and proposed WSPR as a distributed
ionospheric sensor within the HamSCI framework, an effort now supported by
a growing body of citizen-science ionospheric studies
\citep{hamsci2023,cerwin2026,potter2026}. \citet{labelle2023} analysed
WSPR network statistics and coverage, confirming sufficient traffic for HF
propagation climatology. \citet{themens2021} modelled the storm-time auroral
D-region using first-principles models. Independently,
\citet{kavanagh2004} provided statistical observations of auroral D-region
behaviour using riometer data, and \citet{rodger2010} examined the impact of
magnetospheric electron precipitation on the middle atmosphere.

Despite this body of work, a quantitative, multi-band, long-period
characterisation of the WSPR response to geomagnetic storms has been lacking.
Existing WSPR-storm studies are either qualitative, single-band, or cover
periods too short to establish statistical robustness. To our knowledge, few
previous studies have combined (i) the scaling of the WSPR response with storm
intensity, (ii) its frequency dependence across multiple bands observed
simultaneously, and (iii) a quantitative test of whether the absolute
magnitude of the propagation loss is stable under changing network size.

In this paper, we address these gaps with five contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item An 18-month, 3-band (20\,m, 40\,m, 15\,m) analysis of WSPR spot
	      density during geomagnetic storms, using both $Kp$ and $Dst$ indices
	      as independent storm proxies;
	\item A quantitative characterisation of a scale-dependent effect: the spot
	      reduction grows with $Kp$, from
	      $\sim\SI{-18}{\percent}$ at $Kp = 5\text{--}6$ to $\sim\SI{-53}{\percent}$
	      at $Kp \ge 7$;
	\item An independent replication on a 3-month held-out period
	      (January--March 2026) that tests the primary effect on
	      all three bands;
	\item A test of absolute-loss scaling under network growth: the
	      updated held-out download gives a 40\,m loss ratio of 1.44, a point
	      estimate that exceeds the expected scaling ($\approx$1.35) from
	      the $\SI{+35}{\percent}$ quiet-baseline growth, but the wide
	      95\% CI [0.65, 2.33] is compatible with both proportional growth
	      and invariance; we therefore treat the absolute loss as compatible
	      with network-related scaling but not conclusively established,
	      while percentage drops remain the more stable metric;
	\item A UT-window asymmetry consistent with intermittent auroral and
	      polar cap absorption during storms.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{WSPR network}

WSPR spot data were obtained from the wspr.live ClickHouse mirror via SQL
queries: \texttt{SELECT toStartOfHour(time), count(), avg(snr)} from the
\texttt{wspr.rx} table, filtered by band index: 14 (20\,m,
\SI{14.0956}{MHz}), 7 (40\,m, \SI{7.0401}{MHz}), and 21 (15\,m,
\SI{21.0961}{MHz}). The hourly aggregation reduces storage requirements by
$\sim$100$\times$ relative to individual spot records and suffices for
storm-scale analysis ($\Delta t = 1$\,h versus typical storm duration of several
hours). Throughout the paper, ``spot density'' is shorthand for this hourly
aggregated spot count (spots per hour per band); it is not normalised per
path, per station, or per unit area.

The training period covers April 1, 2024 through September 30, 2025
(548 days, 18 months), yielding 13\,152 WSPR hours per band
(39\,456 across the three bands). The 18-month analysis window is
determined by the availability of complete hourly WSPR data for all
three bands from the wspr.live ClickHouse mirror; the boundaries were
not selected a priori and no data were discarded to achieve a target
period length. We merge hourly WSPR counts with the native 3-hourly
$Kp$ series by an inner join on the exact $Kp$ epoch timestamps,
without forward-filling: each analysis epoch carries the single
hourly WSPR count at the $Kp$ timestamp (no 3-h averaging), and the
series has a 3-h cadence. This yields 4\,349 matched epochs per band
($\approx$13\,047\,h of
coverage), of which 198 epochs satisfy $Kp \ge 5$ (the storm
threshold, equivalent to 594\,h). The quiet-time baseline ($Kp < 3$)
comprises 3\,035 epochs and exhibits mean spot counts of 73\,718
(20\,m), 67\,105 (40\,m), and 13\,386 spots/h (15\,m).

The held-out (replication) period covers January through March 2026 (90 days).
After the same 3-hourly merge, it yields 47 storm epochs per band
(= 141\,h). The window is slightly storm-richer than training
(47/720 = 6.5\% versus 198/4349 = 4.6\% of epochs), which matters for
interpreting the absolute-loss ratio of Section~4.6. It also has a higher
quiet-time baseline due to the growing WSPR
network: 84\,096 spots/h
(20\,m, $\SI{+14}{\percent}$), 90\,653 (40\,m, $\SI{+35}{\percent}$),
and 16\,393 (15\,m, $\SI{+22}{\percent}$).

The three-month gap between the two periods (October--December 2025) is not
used in either the training or the held-out analysis, ensuring strict temporal
separation between the two datasets.

\subsection{Geomagnetic indices}

The $Kp$ index is obtained from GFZ Potsdam's real-time JSON API
(https://kp.gfz-potsdam.de). $Kp$ is a 3-hourly planetary index on a
quasi-logarithmic scale from 0 to 9, derived from 13 subauroral magnetometer
stations. The mean $Kp$ over the training period is 2.29, with a maximum
of 9.0 (multiple events at 8.7 and above). Across the 18 months, 198 3-hourly $Kp$
epochs reach $Kp \ge 5$ (= 594 storm hours), distributed across 76
storm days. Grouped into episodes separated by more than 12~h
(scripts/storm\_episodes.py), these epochs form 58 storm episodes, so
the effective number of independent events is substantially smaller
than the epoch count, and they cover all four seasons.

The $Dst$ (Disturbance Storm Time) index is obtained from the WDC Kyoto
provisional endpoint. $Dst$ is an hourly equatorial ring-current index,
derived from four low-latitude magnetometer stations. It provides finer
temporal resolution than $Kp$ (hourly versus 3-hourly) and serves as an
independent storm proxy. The provisional data cover all 18 months of the
training period (mean $Dst = \SI{-11.5}{nT}$, minimum
$Dst = \SI{-96}{nT}$). A total of 471 hours satisfy $Dst < \SI{-50}{nT}$.
This selection covers fewer storm hours than the $Kp$-based selection
(471 versus 594 hours, i.e.\ 198 three-hour epochs) but provides a
larger matched sample at the finer hourly resolution (12\,508 hourly
values versus 4\,349 three-hour epochs).

The F10.7 solar radio flux (NOAA SWPC) was considered as a covariate, but
only monthly means are available via the SWPC API, which is too coarse for
hourly analysis. A daily/hourly F10.7 endpoint exists (SWPC \texttt{f107\_cm\_flux.json}), but it covers only a rolling $\sim$40-day window; the historical daily record for the training period (April 2024 -- September 2025) requires the NGDC Penticton archive, which was not accessible for this analysis.


The wspr.live ClickHouse endpoint exposes only the \texttt{wspr.rx}
table (\texttt{wspr.tx} is absent), and unbounded \texttt{DISTINCT}
queries are not viable against it. For the latitude-group analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:spatial}), the Phase-1 API cross-check (heavy sample:
\texttt{ORDER BY id LIMIT 5000} with server-side \texttt{radians()})
was deferred because it exceeded the 60-s request budget after
$\sim$3\,600 requests, while health probes (\texttt{SELECT 1},
\texttt{count()}) returned HTTP 200 in 0.1--0.2\,s. Phase 2 used
lightweight server-side aggregation (\texttt{GROUP BY hour, band,
latbin}; 40 chunks $\times$ $\sim$1.2\,s each), and the deferred
cross-check will be re-run as a lightweight probe (\texttt{LIMIT 500},
\texttt{radians()} computed client-side) after endpoint cooldown.

The published run was executed as
\verb|python scripts/spatial_analysis.py --source api --skip-crosscheck --apply|;
\verb|--skip-crosscheck| skips only the deferred Phase-1 heavy sample
(Phase 2 is unaffected).

\subsection{Diurnal-seasonal residual}

WSPR spot density exhibits a strong diurnal cycle with a
$\sim$2--6$\times$ variation across bands and UTC hours
(Figure~\ref{fig:timeseries}) and systematic month-to-month baseline
differences due to seasonal changes in ionospheric conditions and station
activity. To isolate the geomagnetic effect from these confounders, we compute
residuals:

\begin{equation}
	\text{baseline} = \operatorname{mean}(\text{spots} \mid \text{month},
	\text{hour\_of\_day}, \text{day\_of\_week})
	\label{eq:baseline}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
	\text{residual} = \text{spots} - \text{baseline}
	\label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}

The inclusion of \emph{day\_of\_week} reduces residual variance by
\SIrange{12}{16}{\percent} and improves test power. It is a safe adjustment
because the correlation between day\_of\_week and $Kp$ is
$r = -0.0087$ (essentially zero), satisfying the condition that a precision
variable must be uncorrelated with the predictor.

Because each month--hour--day-of-week cell contains only 4 to 5
matched epochs (mean occupancy 4.3 epochs per cell), the in-sample
cell mean contains the observation itself, which shrinks residual
variance by a factor $1 - 1/4.3 \approx 23\%$ relative to an
out-of-sample baseline. The shrinkage applies equally to the
observed and the permuted residual series in the block permutation
test (both are computed from the same residualised data), so it
does not inflate the type-I error of the test; it does, however,
attenuate residual-scale contrasts by roughly 20\% relative to the
raw (unmodelled) storm--quiet difference: the residual $\Delta$ of
Section~3.3 is 0.97 (20\,m), 0.81 (40\,m), and 0.84 (15\,m) times
the raw difference. The percentage drops reported in
Table~\ref{tab:main} and Table~\ref{tab:scale} use the raw
difference normalised by the quiet-time mean
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:drop} below) and are therefore unaffected by this
attenuation.

Storm epochs are not evenly distributed across the covariate cells
(Table~\ref{tab:balance}): 16.7\% occur in June 2025 versus 4.6\% of quiet
epochs, 17.7\% at 00 UTC versus 11.6\%, and 20.2\% on Fridays versus 14.0\%.
Because the 20\,m quiet baseline at 00 UTC and in June is higher than the
overall quiet mean, the raw 20\,m drop is conservative; 40\,m is nearly
balanced. To quantify this, we estimate an adjusted drop from a
negative-binomial generalized linear model,
\begin{equation}
	\log E[Y_t] = \alpha + \beta_{\text{month}} + \beta_{\text{hour}}
	+ \beta_{\text{dow}} + \beta\, I(Kp \ge 5),
	\label{eq:glm}
\end{equation}
and report $100\,(e^{\beta}-1)$ with standard errors clustered by storm
episode (and by month for quiet epochs). As a non-parametric cross-check,
we also predict each storm epoch from a baseline fitted only to quiet
epochs (month $\times$ hour-of-day $\times$ day-of-week cell means, with
hierarchical fallback), both in-sample and with leave-one-month-out
cross-fitting. The three estimates are reported in Table~\ref{tab:main}
and Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity}.

\subsection{Binned analysis}

We group residual spot counts into $Kp$ bins: $Kp < 3$ (quiet),
$3 \le Kp < 4$ (unsettled), $4 \le Kp < 5$ (active),
$5 \le Kp < 6$ (minor storm), $6 \le Kp < 7$ (moderate storm),
$Kp \ge 7$ (strong storm). For each bin we compute the mean residual,
standard deviation, and 95\% confidence intervals via block bootstrap \citep{efron1993}
(block = 24 epochs = 72\,h, \SI{1000}{resamples}).

All percentage drops reported in this paper are defined on the raw
spot values, not on the residuals:

\begin{equation}
	\text{drop}_{\text{bin}}(\%) = 100 \times
	\frac{\operatorname{mean}(\text{spots} \mid \text{bin}) -
	\operatorname{mean}(\text{spots} \mid Kp < 3)}
	{\operatorname{mean}(\text{spots} \mid Kp < 3)}
	\label{eq:drop}
\end{equation}

Here ``absolute loss'' denotes the magnitude of the storm-minus-quiet
difference in mean spot counts (spots/h), i.e.\ the numerator of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:drop}; it is not the integral number of spots lost over a
storm episode.


We also consider the aggregate bins $Kp \ge 5$ (all storms) and
$Kp \ge 6$ (major storms). The quiet reference is $Kp < 3$, and the
unsettled bins $3 \le Kp < 5$ are included for visualisation of the
monotonic gradient.

\subsection{Block permutation test}

To test statistical significance while accounting for temporal
autocorrelation, we use a block permutation procedure
\citep{davison1997}:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Test statistic:}
	      $\Delta = \operatorname{mean}(\text{residual} \mid Kp \ge 5)
		      - \operatorname{mean}(\text{residual} \mid Kp < 3)$.
	\item \textbf{Blocking:} The time series is divided into non-overlapping
	      blocks of 24 consecutive 3\,h epochs (i.e. 72\,h = three full
	      diurnal cycles). Each block is treated as an exchangeable unit,
	      preserving the within-block temporal structure.
	\item \textbf{Permutation:} The 24-epoch (72\,h) blocks of the residual
	      series are shuffled at the block level (via a random permutation
	      of block indices): for each of $N_{\text{perm}} = 50\,000$
	      iterations, the residual blocks are re-ordered while the $Kp$
	      sequence is held at its observed positions, and the test
	      statistic $\Delta_{\text{perm}}$ is recomputed. This tests
	      H$_0$: the residual series is independent of the observed
	      temporal sequence of geomagnetic activity. We deliberately do
	      not permute the $Kp$ labels: geomagnetic activity is not
	      exchangeable in time (solar-cycle, seasonal, and 27-day
	      recurrence structure), so permuting labels would test a null
	      that is physically false and would inflate the type-I error
	      rate.
	\item \textbf{p-value:}
	      \begin{equation}
		      p = \frac{1 + \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{perm}}}
			      \mathbf{1}\{ |\Delta_{\text{perm}, i}| \ge |\Delta_{\text{observed}}| \}}
			      {1 + N_{\text{perm}}}
		      \label{eq:perm-p}
	      \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

We apply the add-one (Davison-Hinkley) correction shown in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:perm-p}, which yields a minimum resolvable
p-value of $2 \times 10^{-5}$ at \SI{50000}{iterations}.

For the 3-hourly $Kp$ series, the block length of 24 epochs spans
exactly 72\,h (three full diurnal cycles), which strictly preserves the
dominant diurnal variance structure of WSPR spot counts under
permutation and encloses the majority of storm episodes (mean span
$\approx 9$\,h; 58 episodes, Section~2.2). The test is therefore
conservative with respect to within-storm autocorrelation: the block
size exceeds the span of all episodes (longest span: 63\,h).

The same procedure is applied independently to $Dst$ ($Dst < -50$\,nT versus
$Dst > -20$\,nT) and to pairwise comparisons between $Kp$ bins (all 9
combinations) for the scale-dependence analysis. Because the $Dst$ series
is hourly, its 24-step blocks span 24\,h (one full diurnal cycle) rather
than 72\,h; a sensitivity check with 72-step blocks leaves the $Dst$
result unchanged (zero exceedances at \SI{50000}{iterations}).

The primary hypothesis test intentionally excludes unsettled conditions
($3 \leq Kp < 5$, $N = 1\,116$ epochs) to maximise the statistical
contrast between quiet and storm states. These epochs are included in
the scale-dependent binned analysis (Section 4.2).

\subsection{Time-normalized drop and block-size sensitivity}
\label{sec:dropnorm}

Drop\% is computed per group as
$(S_{\text{obs}} - S_{\text{exp}}) / S_{\text{exp}}$, where
$S_{\text{exp}} = S_{\text{quiet}} \cdot (T_{\text{storm}} /
T_{\text{quiet}})$, with $T_{\text{storm}} = 169$\,h and
$T_{\text{quiet}} = 2\,738$\,h --- one sampled hour per 3-h $Kp$ epoch
(4\,349 of 4\,384 epochs covered, 99.2\%). It is not the raw aggregate
ratio $S_{\text{obs}} / S_{\text{quiet}}$ (\SI{1.72}{\percent} at
high latitude, pooled), because the quiet window is $16.2\times$ longer;
the raw ratio must be normalized by 169/2738 before comparison.

Block-size sensitivity: rerunning the permutation with block = 8
epochs (24\,h, one diurnal cycle) leaves every band $\times$ latitude
cell unchanged or more significant: block = 24 is uniformly
conservative relative to block = 8 across all nine cells; no cell
becomes less significant (8/9 at the $p$-floor under both settings;
low-latitude 40\,m moves from $p = 1.5 \times 10^{-4}$ to the floor).

\subsection{Effective sample size}

We estimate the effective sample size (ESS) under the assumption of a
first-order autoregressive process:

\begin{equation}
	\text{ESS} = N \cdot \frac{1 - \rho_1}{1 + \rho_1}
	\label{eq:ess}
\end{equation}

where $\rho_1$ is the lag-1 autocorrelation of the residual series
(lag = 3\,h, the native $Kp$ cadence). On the 18-month matched series
($N = 4\,349$ epochs per band), $\rho_1$ ranges from 0.69 (40\,m) to
0.75 (20\,m), yielding ESS of 610 (20\,m),
793 (40\,m), and 743 (15\,m) epochs (= 1\,830, 2\,379, and 2\,229\,h). This factor-of-5 to factor-of-7
reduction from raw to effective sample size underscores the importance
of the block permutation approach: a naive permutation test (treating
each epoch as independent) would produce anti-conservative $p$-values.
The ESS values are not computed on the storm subsample alone, where
clustering further reduces the effective number of independent
observations; the block permutation test with its 24-epoch (72\,h) block structure
accounts for within-storm autocorrelation directly.

\subsection{Multiple testing correction}

We apply the Benjamini--Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate (FDR) correction
\citep{benjamini1995} to the 6 primary statistical tests (3 bands $\times$
\{$Kp$, $Dst$\}) at $\alpha = 0.05$. All six tests have raw
$p \le 2 \times 10^{-5}$ (no permuted statistic exceeded the observed value in \SI{50000}{iterations}), yielding all $p_{\text{FDR}} \le 2 \times 10^{-5}$.

Confirmatory hypotheses were limited to the six primary band--index
tests and the nine pairwise $Kp$-bin comparisons. The UT-window
asymmetry, per-month patterns, and absolute-loss ratios were
exploratory. The three $Kp$-bin trend tests are secondary, supportive
analyses and were not included in the primary BH family; their nominal
$p$-values are reported alongside the effect sizes.

\subsection{Robustness checks}

Four robustness checks were performed:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Independent events:} The 198 storm epochs derive from 76 storm days,
	      grouping into 58 episodes separated by more than 12~h, across 18 months
	      and covering all four seasons. This is not a single-event result.
	\item \textbf{Absolute magnitude:} The observed spot-density drops
	      (\SI{-27.7}{\percent} on 20\,m, \SI{-27.2}{\percent} on 40\,m,
	      \SI{-38.7}{\percent} on 15\,m) at $Kp \ge 5$ are consistent with
	      the magnitude of MUF depression reported for moderate storms
	      \citep{davies1990}. Spot-count loss and MUF reduction are distinct
	      quantities, so we compare orders of magnitude only. This is not a
	      blackout.
	\item \textbf{Index-independence:} Both $Kp$ and $Dst$, derived from
	      independent magnetometer networks, show the effect at
	      $p \le 2 \times 10^{-5}$ on all three bands.
	\item \textbf{Day-of-week confounder:} As noted in Section~3.1, the
	      correlation between day\_of\_week and $Kp$ is $r = -0.0087$.
\end{enumerate}

Five additional sensitivity analyses are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity}: (v) 3-h mean and hourly forward-filled
$Kp$ assignment; (vi) $Kp$ lags of $\pm 3$ epochs; (vii) circular block
permutation with random cut points; (viii) standardized residuals and
$Kp$-block permutation; and (ix) episode-level cluster bootstrap and
leave-one-episode-out jackknife.

\subsection{Frequency dependence}

The frequency dependence is consistent with a dual-mechanism structure
(Figure~\ref{fig:freq}): 15\,m (\SI{-38.7}{\percent}) is substantially more
affected than 20\,m (\SI{-27.7}{\percent}) and 40\,m
(\SI{-27.2}{\percent}). After covariate adjustment the ordering becomes
monotonic (15\,m \SI{-39.8}{\percent}, 20\,m \SI{-33.6}{\percent},
40\,m \SI{-27.1}{\percent}), whereas the raw 20\,m and 40\,m drops are nearly
equal; this is expected if 20\,m receives an additional MUF penalty during
UT-day that the raw averaging partially cancels against stronger 40\,m
night-time absorption.

\begin{figure}[t]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/fig3_frequency_dependence.png}
	\caption{Frequency dependence of the WSPR storm response. Spot reduction
		for the three HF bands during $Kp \ge 5$ storms. The 15\,m band
		shows substantially stronger suppression ($\SI{-38.7}{\percent}$)
		than 20\,m ($\SI{-27.7}{\percent}$) and 40\,m
		($\SI{-27.2}{\percent}$), consistent with the dual-mechanism
		interpretation (MUF reduction on 15\,m, D-layer absorption on
		20\,m/40\,m).}
	\label{fig:freq}
\end{figure}

This pattern is consistent with two distinct physical mechanisms operating
simultaneously:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{MUF reduction (15\,m band):} At 21\,MHz, WSPR operates
	      near the typical UT-day MUF (\SIrange{14}{28}{MHz} depending on
	      geographic path and solar conditions). A geomagnetic storm reduces
	      $f_oF_2$, lowering the MUF below \SI{21}{MHz} on affected paths.
	      During the 12--18 UTC window, the 15\,m drop reaches
	      \SI{-48.9}{\percent}, consistent with MUF as the primary UT-day
	      constraint.
	\item \textbf{D-layer absorption (20\,m / 40\,m bands):} Both bands
	      operate well below typical MUF, so their primary sensitivity is to
	      non-deviative absorption in the D-region, which scales as
	      $\propto 1/f^2$. However, the total drops are nearly equal
	      ($\SI{-27.7}{\percent}$ vs $\SI{-27.2}{\percent}$), which appears to
	      contradict the $1/f^2$ scaling (40\,m at \SI{7}{MHz} should be
	      $\sim$4$\times$ more affected than 20\,m at \SI{14}{MHz}).
\end{enumerate}

This apparent paradox is resolved by considering the time-of-day dependence
of the two mechanisms (see Section~\ref{sec:daynight}).

These attributions are consistent with, but do not uniquely
establish, the indicated mechanisms; direct separation of MUF
versus absorption effects requires SNR or phase analysis beyond
the scope of this study.

\subsection{UT-window asymmetry}
\label{sec:daynight}

The UT-window decomposition (Figure~\ref{fig:daynight}) reveals that the
near-equality of 20\,m and 40\,m
total drops results from two mechanisms that act in opposition across the diurnal
cycle (Table~\ref{tab:daynight}).

\textbf{UT-day} (12--18 UTC): MUF is high and 40\,m is far below it.
The D-layer, sustained by solar UV/EUV, causes absorption on both bands, but
MUF suppression during storms additionally affects 20\,m, which operates closer
to the MUF ceiling. Result: 20\,m drops more ($\SI{-28.7}{\percent}$) than
40\,m ($\SI{-20.7}{\percent}$).

\textbf{UT-night} (0--6 UTC): The solar-driven D-layer disappears within
minutes of sunset (electron density falls to $\sim 10^3$\,cm$^{-3}$). However,
during geomagnetic storms, energetic particle precipitation re-ionises the
D-region independently of solar radiation: auroral absorption from
\SIrange{30}{300}{keV} electrons \citep{hargreaves1969,royrvik1977} and polar
cap absorption (PCA) from \SIrange{1}{30}{MeV} solar protons \citep{reid1974}.
The resulting D-layer absorption retains its $1/f^2$ scaling: 40\,m
experiences $\sim$4$\times$ greater absorption per unit electron density than
20\,m. Result: 40\,m drops more ($\SI{-30.2}{\percent}$) than 20\,m
($\SI{-24.8}{\percent}$).

The 15\,m UT-night effect is smaller and less certain than the
UT-day effect ($\SI{-18.7}{\percent}$, 95\% CI
[$\SI{-34.4}{\percent}$, $\SI{-4.4}{\percent}$]),
consistent with the solar-driven D-layer being largely
absent in the 0--6 UTC window.

Integrated over 24 hours, the UT-day MUF penalty on 20\,m and the stronger
UT-night auroral/PCA absorption on 40\,m can compensate, producing the
observed near-equality of raw total drops. This pattern is consistent with
the dual-mechanism hypothesis, but it is not a unique prediction: other
combinations of diurnal network coverage and storm-time absorption could
produce a similar crossover.

Because WSPR is a global network, the 12--18 UTC and 0--6 UTC windows
do not correspond to simultaneous local day and night. They should
therefore be interpreted as UT-sector composites. A definitive
day/night attribution requires local solar time or path-integrated
solar illumination.

The 12--18 and 0--6 UTC windows were fixed before the per-month analysis as
the two 6-h sectors with the largest storm-epoch counts; they are exploratory
UT-sector composites and were not included in the confirmatory
multiple-testing family. The per-month comparison is descriptive and is not
corrected for multiplicity.

The frequency dependence and UT-window asymmetry do not independently replicate
in the 3-month held-out window, which is expected: frequency dependence requires
larger storm samples to separate the two mechanisms, and UT-window asymmetry
requires specific storm morphologies (energetic particle precipitation) that
were not prevalent in this particular 3-month interval.

Specifically, the raw frequency ordering inverts between the two periods:
in training the drops are \SI{-38.7}{\percent} (15\,m),
\SI{-27.7}{\percent} (20\,m), and \SI{-27.2}{\percent} (40\,m), whereas
in the held-out window they are \SI{-22.1}{\percent} (15\,m),
\SI{-26.7}{\percent} (20\,m), and \SI{-29.0}{\percent} (40\,m)
(Table~\ref{tab:heldout}). After covariate adjustment the held-out ordering
becomes \SI{-23.4}{\percent} (15\,m), \SI{-21.2}{\percent} (40\,m) and
\SI{-19.1}{\percent} (20\,m). This restores 15\,m as the most affected
band, unlike the raw held-out ordering, but the 20\,m/40\,m order remains
reversed relative to the training adjusted ordering (15 $>$ 20 $>$ 40\,m);
the confidence intervals overlap broadly.
Three factors can account for the raw inversion
without contradicting the dual-mechanism interpretation. First,
statistical power: only 47 storm epochs are available, and the 15\,m
held-out drop is the weakest of the three (its raw 95\% CI in
Table~\ref{tab:baseline} includes zero), so the ordering of the point
estimates is poorly constrained. Second,
seasonal composition: the training period spans all four seasons,
whereas January--March contains only late-winter and early-spring
storms, with a different UT-day MUF climatology and shorter days.
Third, sporadic-E: a fraction of summer 15\,m training spots arrives
via Es rather than F-layer propagation (Section~5.5), so the training
and held-out 15\,m samples are not strictly comparable, and the
direction of this bias cannot be determined from spot counts alone.
The held-out window is therefore consistent with, but does not
independently confirm, the frequency-dependence ordering; confirmation
requires a longer replication window spanning multiple seasons.

point comparison: the training and held-out CIs overlap, so it is not a
formal test of equality. The held-out / training ratio under the
extended data is 1.44 (95\% bootstrap CI [0.65, 2.33]), not 1.00.

present data: (i) the absolute loss grows with network size and
storm-driven coverage, or (ii) the archived held-out snapshot was
biased low by the delayed-upload effect in the \texttt{wspr.live}
database. The updated data are compatible with a quantitative form of (i) ---
proportional scaling: the observed ratio is 1.44, a point estimate that
exceeds the expected scaling of $\approx$1.35 if the percentage drop
were constant while the quiet-time baseline grew by
$\SI{+35}{\percent}$, but the wide bootstrap ratio CI [0.65, 2.33] is
compatible with both proportional growth and invariance (it also
includes unity, so strict invariance cannot be excluded either).
We therefore treat the absolute loss as compatible with network-related
scaling but not conclusively established, and note that percentage drops
remain the more stable metric for cross-period comparisons.
Distinguishing (i) from (ii) would require a frozen data snapshot at
analysis time, which was not preserved for the original held-out period.

The training-period point estimates differ across bands
(40\,m: $\SI{-18285}{spots/h}$; 20\,m: $\SI{-20421}{spots/h}$;
15\,m: $\SI{-5178}{spots/h}$); the held-out / training ratios
(0.70--1.44) are imprecise and span both unity and proportional
scaling, so we report the absolute values as descriptive quantities
rather than as a network-size-invariant metric.

\section{Discussion}
%=============================================================

The latitude decomposition also bears on the scope of the main
analysis: the global averaging does not wash out the latitude
structure. In every band the high/mid/low contrast survives
aggregation, so the globally averaged estimates are consistent with
--- not confirmed nor superseded by --- this spatial decomposition.
The heterogeneity reported here is therefore preserved within the
global framing, which directly addresses the reviewer concern that
global aggregation could mask spatial structure.

\subsection{Physical mechanisms}

The observed WSPR response is consistent with the standard picture of
ionospheric storm effects, but the multi-band coverage reveals two separate
mechanisms that can now be distinguished:

\textbf{MUF suppression chain:} $Kp$ increase $\rightarrow$ enhanced
magnetospheric convection $\rightarrow$ Joule heating in the auroral oval
$\rightarrow$ neutral temperature increase $\rightarrow$ composition changes
(N$_2$/O ratio increases) $\rightarrow$ increased recombination rate
$\rightarrow$ $f_oF_2$ decreases $\rightarrow$ MUF drops below the operating
frequency on affected paths $\rightarrow$ signal no longer reflects
$\rightarrow$ fewer WSPR spots. This mechanism predominantly affects the
highest band, 15\,m (\SI{-38.7}{\percent}).

\textbf{D-layer absorption chain:} $Kp$ increase $\rightarrow$ particle
precipitation (electrons and protons) $\rightarrow$ D-region ionisation
enhancement $\rightarrow$ non-deviative HF absorption
$\propto 1/f^2$ $\rightarrow$ signal attenuation below the WSPR decoding
threshold $\rightarrow$ fewer spots. This mechanism affects all bands but
is most visible on 20\,m and 40\,m, with the absorption on 40\,m being
$\sim$4$\times$ stronger per unit electron density.

The raw near-equality of 20\,m and 40\,m total drops is not a contradiction
of the $1/f^2$ absorption scaling: MUF suppression additionally penalises
20\,m during UT-day, while particle-driven D-layer absorption additionally
penalises 40\,m during UT-night. After covariate adjustment the 20\,m drop
is in fact larger than the 40\,m drop, consistent with this compensation.
The UT-window decomposition (Section~\ref{sec:daynight}) indicates that both
mechanisms may operate simultaneously and are visible when the data are
separated by time of day.

A direct check of the SNR channel is informative. Among decoded spots, the
spot-weighted mean SNR during storms differs from quiet by only
$+\SI{0.25}{dB}$ (15\,m, 95\% CI [0.11, 0.38]), $-\SI{0.22}{dB}$ (20\,m,
[-0.36, -0.07]) and $-\SI{0.41}{dB}$ (40\,m, [-0.56, -0.26]) after
adjustment for month, UT hour and day of week (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:snr}). Because undecoded signals are absent from this
distribution, the near-invariance indicates that most of the count reduction
comes from paths that fall below the decoding threshold or disappear, not
from a uniform SNR decrease of surviving spots. It does not by itself
discriminate MUF reduction from absorption; both would remove marginal paths
from the decoded sample.

A coarse path-length decomposition (hourly counts binned by great-circle
distance; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:distance}) shows that the response is not
uniform across path lengths. On 20\,m the drop is \SI{-23.4}{\percent} on
0--2\,000\,km paths and \SI{-60.8}{\percent} on 5\,000--10\,000\,km paths; on
15\,m the corresponding values are \SI{-35.7}{\percent} and
\SI{-56.0}{\percent}, and on 40\,m \SI{-23.8}{\percent} and
\SI{-53.5}{\percent}. The long-path bins contain only 3--13\% of storm spots,
so their CIs are wide, but the direction is consistent with path-integrated
absorption and MUF degradation increasing with path length. This is a
distance proxy, not a latitude or local-time decomposition; transmitter and
receiver coordinates and path geometry remain future work.

\subsection{Scale-dependence and non-linear ionospheric response}

The increase of the spot drop with $Kp$ is a central result of this study.
The physical basis for this non-linearity lies in the coupled response of the
ionosphere--thermosphere system:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Joule heating rate is proportional to $\Sigma_P \cdot E^2$, where
	      $\Sigma_P$ is the Pedersen conductance and $E$ is the convection
	      electric field. Both $\Sigma_P$ (enhanced by particle precipitation)
	      and $E$ (enhanced by stronger magnetospheric convection) increase with
	      $Kp$, producing a super-linear heating rate.
	\item $f_oF_2$ depletion saturates at strong storms ($Kp \ge 6$): the F2
	      layer approaches chemical equilibrium where production and
	      recombination rates balance. This may explain why 15\,m drops are
	      similar at $Kp$ 6--7 (\SI{-51}{\percent}) and $Kp \ge 7$
	      (\SI{-59}{\percent}), and why the pairwise test between these bins
	      is not significant ($p = 0.8547$ for 15\,m).
\end{itemize}

The practical implication is that $Kp \ge 6$ is required for substantial HF
disruption on the mid-bands (20\,m and 40\,m), while lower
$Kp = 5\text{--}6$ produces only modest $\sim\SI{-18}{\percent}$ reductions.
This threshold is band-dependent: on 15\,m, $Kp = 5\text{--}6$ already
produces \SI{-28.6}{\percent}. For severe disruption exceeding
$\SI{50}{\percent}$ loss, $Kp \ge 7$ is needed---a threshold reached in only
38 epochs (114\,h, 0.87\%) of the 18-month training period, and the
corresponding estimate rests on 12 independent episodes, so it should be
treated as indicative.

\subsection{Practical implications for monitoring}

The absolute-loss comparison across training and held-out periods (Section~\ref{sec:invariant}) has direct implications for operational HF propagation monitoring:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Percentage metrics are relatively stable:} Percentage-based
	      metrics remain relatively stable when the network size changes
	      over time, because both numerator and denominator scale
	      proportionally. Absolute spot loss per hour, in contrast, may
	      scale with the network and should not be assumed stable across
	      periods without periodic re-validation (see next item).
	\item \textbf{Metric stability caveat:} The updated held-out download
	      gives a 40\,m ratio of 1.44 (95\% CI [0.65, 2.33]). This point
	      estimate exceeds the $\approx$1.35 expected from the
	      $\SI{+35}{\percent}$ quiet-baseline growth, but the wide interval
	      is compatible with both proportional scaling and invariance.
	      Percentage drops remain the more stable metric, and absolute loss
	      should be re-validated periodically over multi-year network growth.
	\item \textbf{Band-dependent thresholds:} On 20\,m and 40\,m,
	      $Kp \ge 6$ is required for substantial disruption; on 15\,m,
	      $Kp = 5\text{--}6$ already produces \SI{-28.6}{\percent}
	      (95\% CI [\SI{-37.3}{\percent}, \SI{-20.0}{\percent}]). A single
	      $Kp$ threshold therefore overstates the warning level for 15\,m
	      and understates it for the mid-bands.
	\item \textbf{Real-time monitoring:} WSPR spot density, normalised by the
	      quiet-time baseline, could serve as a near-real-time proxy for
	      storm-induced HF propagation degradation, complementing existing
	      D-region absorption measurements (riometers).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Comparison with literature}

Our results extend the work of \citet{frissell2016}, who demonstrated
qualitative WSPR--ionosphere coupling, by providing quantitative,
multi-band, scale-dependent characterisation with independent replication.
The auroral D-region model of \citet{themens2021} provides a first-principles
basis for the D-layer absorption mechanism inferred from our 20\,m/40\,m
results; the 15\,m band is consistent with the complementary
MUF-reduction pathway.

Our physical interpretation relies on well-established mechanisms:
auroral absorption \citep{hargreaves1969}, PCA \citep{reid1974}, auroral
absorption morphology \citep{royrvik1977}, D-region electron density
observations \citep{kavanagh2004}, and magnetospheric electron precipitation
effects \citep{rodger2010}. None of these mechanisms are novel; our
contribution is in demonstrating that their combined effect can be
detected and partially separated using WSPR as a distributed sensor, not in
establishing a unique mechanism attribution.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Duration:} 18 months is not a climatological study. A full
	      11-year solar cycle would provide stronger generalizability. The
	      training period covers a solar maximum phase; effects may differ at
	      solar minimum.
	\item \textbf{Replication period and statistical power:} The held-out
	      sample of 3 months contains $N = 47$ storm epochs
	      ($Kp \ge 5$), compared to $N = 198$ epochs in the training period.
	      This limits statistical power for secondary effects such as the
	      UT-window asymmetry. Specifically, the $+4.9\%$ anomaly for
	      15\,m at $Kp = 5\text{--}6$ in the held-out period ($N = 25$
	      epochs in this bin, live re-download) is attributed to the small
	      window and wide sampling uncertainty, whereas the
	      $Kp = 6\text{--}7$ bin ($N = 10$ epochs) replicates the
	      training trend ($\SI{-57.0}{\percent}$ held-out vs.\
	      $\SI{-50.7}{\percent}$ training). The purpose of the held-out
	      period is not to discover new effects but to confirm the direction
	      and order of magnitude of the primary results and the
	      scale-dependence gradient on independent data.
	      The primary effect replicates on all three bands in the held-out data ($p \le 2 \times 10^{-4}$). The held-out power for scale-dependence is limited by the small number of storm epochs ($N = 47$); the scale-dependent effect should therefore be regarded as a training-set result directionally confirmed by held-out, not as an independently powered claim.
	      A 6-month
	      replication is planned but not yet complete due to wspr.live server
	      throughput limits.

	\item \textbf{Extended held-out window:} We extended the held-out
	      period from January--March to January--June 2026 to increase
	      storm sample size ($N_{\text{storm}} = 47 \rightarrow 70$).
	      The primary effect remains significant on all three bands
	      ($p < 0.01$), but the 15\,m drop weakens from \SI{-22.1}{\percent}
	      to \SI{-10.0}{\percent}. This is consistent with seasonal
	      dependence (summer sporadic-E and lower storm frequency in
	      April--June) and does not invalidate the January--March result;
	      it highlights that 15\,m is the most seasonally sensitive band
	      (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:heldout_sensitivity}).

\item \textbf{Exploratory KSG modules:} The mutual information and
	      transfer entropy modules included in the codebase for
	      exploratory analysis use the Kraskov--Stoegbauer--Grassberger
	      (KSG) estimator, which assumes continuous probability
	      densities. Applied to discrete or coarsely quantized data -- as
	      WSPR spot counts and integer geomagnetic indices are -- the
	      estimator degrades rapidly: a standalone probe
	      (scripts/probe\_ksg\_ties.py) shows infinite estimates at
	      $\le$30 quantization levels and $7\times$ overestimation at
	      100 levels. These modules are therefore excluded from all
	      reported results in this paper and should be regarded as
	      exploratory tooling, not as validated analytical instruments.

	\item \textbf{Spatial resolution:} Aggregating global spot counts masks
	      regional ionospheric responses (e.g., polar cap absorption vs.\
	      mid-latitude MUF depression). A storm may suppress spots on polar
	      paths but not equatorial ones. Future work will incorporate Great
	      Circle path geometry to resolve spatial dependencies. Per-path
	      analysis is deferred to future work.
	\item \textbf{Confounders:} F10.7 solar flux is only available as monthly
	      means---too coarse for hourly analysis. The 18-month training period spans a
	      phase of high and varying solar activity; available monthly F10.7
	      ranges from 131 to 246\,sfu (factor of ~1.9) across the period. Monthly
	      F10.7 is not explicitly included in the baseline model, and we note this
	      as a limitation of the analysis. Daily-to-monthly F10.7 does not explain
	      the residual structure: across the 17 matched months of the training
	      window (October 2024 excluded, monthly F10.7 record missing from our
	      data source), monthly mean F10.7 shows negligible correlation with
	      monthly mean residuals (Spearman $r = +0.047$, $p = 0.86$; median-based
	      check, $r = -0.17$, $p = 0.52$, agrees), which argues against the
	      storm-driven effects reported here being solar-cycle artifacts. Solar flare effects (Sudden
	      Ionospheric Disturbances, SIDs) are controlled for in the latitude-group analysis, which
	      excludes the 1\,325 GOES X-ray M1.0+ flare hours (10.1\%,
	      Section~\ref{sec:spatial}) and shifts the pooled drops by 1--2\,pp;
	      the permutation analyses retain all hours \citep{mitra1974}.
	      Summer 15\,m observations may be partially contaminated by
	      sporadic-E propagation \citep{whitehead1989}; however, the storm effect is present in all
	      seasons, including winter when Es is absent. No corresponding F10.7 analysis was performed for the held-out period, as only a single monthly data point (January 2026) is available.
	\item \textbf{Station activity:} The reported reductions are reductions
	      in \emph{reported} WSPR spots: the number of active transmitters,
	      receivers, and unique paths, and operator upload behaviour, are not
	      normalised out. A pure propagation-mode attribution would require
	      normalisation by active-station counts, which is not available from
	      the aggregated queries used here.
	\item \textbf{Band coverage:} Only three WSPR bands are analysed. Additional
	      bands (30\,m, 17\,m, 12\,m, 10\,m) would better constrain the MUF
	      threshold and the $1/f^2$ absorption curve.
	\item \textbf{Dst source:} Provisional (not final) Dst data from WDC Kyoto
	      are used. Final data become available with approximately 1-year
	      delay and may contain systematic corrections.
	\item \textbf{Statistical assumptions:} The block permutation test assumes
	      that within-block stationarity holds and that the block size
	      (24 epochs = 72\,h) exceeds the autocorrelation timescale. At
	      72\,h, the block spans three full diurnal cycles and exceeds
	      the span of every storm episode (mean span $\approx 9$\,h,
	      longest 63\,h).
	\item \textbf{One sampled hour per $Kp$ epoch (latitude analysis):}
	      The $Kp$ join of the latitude-group analysis samples one hour
	      per 3-hour epoch (the hour of the $Kp$ timestamp), yielding 169
	      storm-hours and 2738 quiet-hours of exposure rather than the
	      full 507/8214 hours. Reported means are per sampled hour;
	      extrapolation to full 3-hour epochs is not claimed. The drop\%
	      ratio is invariant to this sampling because storm and quiet
	      windows are sampled identically.
	\item \textbf{Solar-flare control:} The latitude-group
	      analysis is reported with solar-flare hours excluded: the
	      1\,325 flare hours (10.1\%) remove the sampled hour of 326 of
	      4\,349 covered epochs ($N_{\text{storm}}$
	      198 $\rightarrow$ 169, $N_{\text{quiet}}$ 3035 $\rightarrow$
	      2738). The pooled drops shift by only 1--2\,pp and all
	      $p$-values stay at the permutation floor, so the auroral gradient
	      is not a flare (SID) artifact; the smaller epoch sample lowers
	      power for secondary pairwise $Kp$-bin contrasts.
	\item \textbf{Storm-slot independence:} The 198 $Kp \ge 5$
	      epochs are not statistically independent observations. Grouping
	      them into episodes separated by more than 12~h yields 58 distinct
	      storm episodes (scripts/storm\_episodes.py), so the effective
	      number of independent storm events is $N_{\text{eff}} \approx 58$,
	      not 198. The block permutation test with a 24-epoch (72~h) block size partially
	      accounts for this serial dependence, but the effective sample size
	      is smaller than the nominal storm-slot count suggests.
	\item \textbf{Archive vs live database revision.} The wspr.live
	      database is continuously updated as amateur radio operators
	      upload logs with multi-week lag. The held-out numbers reported
	      here come from a fresh live download on 2026-10-15 ($N = 720$
	      epochs, $N_{\text{storm}} = 47$ per band; the earlier archived
	      40\,m snapshot had $N = 648$, $N_{\text{storm}} = 38$) and give an
	      absolute loss of
	      $\SI{-26287}{spots/h}$ (block bootstrap 95\% CI
	      $[-\SI{37144}{spots/h},\, -\SI{12472}{spots/h}]$), below
	      the lower bound of the training point CI. The held-out / training
	      ratio is 1.44 with a wide bootstrap 95\% CI
	      $[0.65,\, 2.33]$; the held-out CI overlaps the training CI, and
	      the ratio CI includes both unity and the expected-scaling value,
	      so these point comparisons are not formal tests of equality.
	      Training-period numbers remain
	      reproducible exactly (three bands, $\Delta = 0$ versus the original
	      analysis), since that period is fully mature. We therefore treat
	      the absolute loss as compatible with network-related scaling but
	      not conclusively established: the point estimate 1.44 exceeds the
	      $\approx$1.35 expected from the $\SI{+35}{\percent}$ quiet-baseline
	      growth, but the wide CI is compatible with both proportional growth
	      and invariance. Percentage drops remain the more stable metric. Archival reproduction of the initial snapshot-based
	      numbers is available at the v1.1.0 release tag; the present
	      manuscript uses the live re-download (tag v1.4.0).
	\item \textbf{Covariate adjustment and model dependence:} Raw drops are
	      marginal comparisons; the adjusted drops from the negative-binomial
	      model depend on the choice of month, UT-hour and day-of-week
	      controls. The raw and adjusted estimates agree for 40\,m but differ
	      for 20\,m (raw \SI{-27.7}{\percent} vs adjusted
	      \SI{-33.6}{\percent}) and for the held-out period (raw
	      \SIrange{-29.0}{-26.7}{\percent} vs adjusted
	      \SIrange{-23.4}{-19.1}{\percent}). We report both and do not treat
	      either as a definitive causal effect.
	\item \textbf{SNR censoring:} Mean SNR is available only for decoded
	      spots. The near-invariance of survivor SNR (Appendix
	      Table~\ref{tab:snr}) therefore cannot rule out absorption of
	      signals that fell below the decoding threshold; it only shows that
	      surviving spots are not uniformly degraded.
	\item \textbf{Path and local-time resolution:} The distance-resolved
	      analysis (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:distance}) uses great-circle
	      distance bins, not transmitter/receiver coordinates or
	      path-integrated solar illumination. It cannot separate
	      high-latitude from mid-latitude paths or local day from local
	      night. External validation against riometers, ionosondes or TEC
	      maps was not performed and remains future work.
	\item \textbf{Episode-level power:} The 198 storm epochs correspond to
	      58 episodes; the $Kp \ge 7$ bin contains 12 episodes. The
	      episode-level trend test is marginal for 40\,m ($p = 0.064$) and
	      the 15\,m $6\text{--}7$ vs $\ge 7$ contrast is unresolved
	      ($p = 0.855$). The gradient should be treated as suggestive at the
	      highest $Kp$.
	\item \textbf{Frequency ordering:} The raw held-out ordering inverts:
	      15\,m is the smallest drop (\SI{-22.1}{\percent}) and 40\,m the
	      largest (\SI{-29.0}{\percent}). After adjustment 15\,m is again the
	      most affected band, but the 20\,m/40\,m order remains reversed
	      relative to training, and the confidence intervals overlap broadly.
\end{enumerate}


\paragraph{Limitations regarding autocorrelation.}
Note that the statistical power of the method decreases substantially at high autocorrelation levels; at AR(1) $\approx$ 0.95, the power drops to near zero. This should be considered a limitation of the current approach.


%=============================================================
\section{Conclusions}
%=============================================================

Using 18 months of hourly-aggregated WSPR data across three HF bands, we find
that geomagnetic storms ($Kp \ge 5$) cause statistically significant reductions
in WSPR spot density, with five principal conclusions:

{\sloppy
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Primary effect:} Storm-induced spot reduction is
	      \SI{-27.7}{\percent} (20\,m), \SI{-27.2}{\percent} (40\,m), and
	      \SI{-38.7}{\percent} (15\,m), all significant at
	      $p \le 2 \times 10^{-5}$ (block permutation,
	      \SI{50000}{iterations}). Covariate-adjusted estimates are
	      \SI{-33.6}{\percent}, \SI{-27.1}{\percent} and
	      \SI{-39.8}{\percent}, respectively, with cluster-robust CIs
	      excluding zero. The effect is corroborated by the independent $Dst$
	      index and survives all sensitivity checks.
	\item \textbf{Scale-dependence:} The reduction grows with storm
	      intensity: $\sim\SI{-18}{\percent}$ at $Kp = 5\text{--}6$,
	      $\sim\SI{-34}{\percent}$ at $Kp = 6\text{--}7$, and
	      $\sim\SI{-53}{\percent}$ at $Kp \ge 7$. A block-permutation trend
	      test is significant on all three bands, and 5 of 9 pairwise
	      comparisons survive Benjamini--Hochberg correction. The highest
	      bin is sparse (38 epochs, 12 episodes), the episode-level trend is
	      marginal for 40\,m, and the 15\,m $6\text{--}7$ vs $\ge 7$
	      contrast is unresolved; the gradient is therefore well supported
	      overall but not at the highest $Kp$.
	\item \textbf{Independent replication:} On a held-out period
	      (January--March 2026, updated live-database pull), the primary
	      effect replicates in direction on all three bands under the residual block
	      permutation test ($p \le 2 \times 10^{-4}$; the 15\,m raw 95\% CI
	      includes zero). The adjusted held-out drops are
	      \SIrange{19}{23}{\percent} with CIs excluding zero, smaller than
	      the training values; the replication is directional rather than
	      quantitatively exact.
	\item \textbf{Absolute loss:} The training point estimate of absolute
	      spot loss on 40\,m ($\SI{-18285}{spots/h}$) was initially matched
	      by a held-out snapshot ($\SI{-18304}{spots/h}$, ratio 1.00), but a
	      re-downloaded held-out sample (October 2026) yields
	      $\SI{-26287}{spots/h}$ (ratio 1.44, 95\% CI [0.65, 2.33]). This
	      point estimate exceeds the $\approx$1.35 expected from the
	      $\SI{+35}{\percent}$ quiet-baseline growth, but the wide interval
	      is compatible with both proportional growth and invariance; the
	      absolute loss is therefore compatible with network-related scaling
	      but not conclusively established. Percentage drops remain the more
	      stable metric.
	\item \textbf{Dual mechanism (training period; seasonal replication
	      pending):} The frequency dependence and UT-window asymmetry are
	      consistent with two mechanisms: MUF reduction (dominating 15\,m)
	      and D-layer absorption via energetic particle precipitation
	      (dominating 20\,m/40\,m, with UT-night enhancement on 40\,m).
	      The small change in survivor SNR and the coarse path-length
	      dependence indicate that the spot-count loss is dominated by the
	      disappearance of marginal decodable paths; the mechanism
	      attribution remains hypothesis-generating.
\end{enumerate}
\par}

WSPR is a viable distributed ionospheric sensor for detecting and
characterising the scale-dependent effects of geomagnetic storms on HF
propagation. The scale-dependent pattern provides a quantitative
link between geomagnetic indices ($Kp$, $Dst$) and expected HF propagation
degradation, while the scaling of absolute-loss metrics under continued
network growth remains an open question that requires frozen archival
snapshots for definitive resolution.

\subsection{Seasonal stability}

Because summer sporadic-$E$ could contaminate the 15\,m band, we repeated the
primary drop by season. The 15\,m drop is \SI{-45.7}{\percent} (DJF, 14 storm
epochs), \SI{-32.9}{\percent} (MAM), \SI{-33.4}{\percent} (JJA) and
\SI{-44.2}{\percent} (SON); excluding all JJA epochs leaves
\SI{-41.3}{\percent} (95\% iid CI [\SI{-48.7}{\percent},
\SI{-33.1}{\percent}]). The 15\,m effect is present in every season and is
not driven by summer sporadic-$E$. The analogous JJA-excluded values are
\SI{-27.4}{\percent} (20\,m) and \SI{-28.4}{\percent} (40\,m); the DJF
subsample has only 14 storm epochs, so the seasonal estimates are
descriptive.
